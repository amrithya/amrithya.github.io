A post about explainability in AI

Created on October 10, 2025

2025   ·   xAI   ·   tech-posts

Introduction

“Trust, but verify.” – Ronald Reagan

This famous quote captures the core problem of modern artificial intelligence. Today, AI systems are used to make important decisions in healthcare, finance, hiring, and science. While many of these systems are highly accurate, they are often difficult or even impossible for humans to understand because of their complex computations. Trusting a model’s output without knowing why it made a decision can be risky, especially when mistakes have real consequences.

This is where Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) comes in. XAI focuses on making our AI systems more transparent, understandable, and accountable. It helps us answer critical questions: Why did the model make this decision? Can we trust it? And who is responsible when it gets things wrong?

To understand interpretability and explainability in a simple and intuitive way, let’s begin with a story.

In a galaxy far, far away…

Luke Skywalker was walking through a space station after a long day of training. He was tired, his stomach was growling, and all he could think about was his favourite snack from the vending machine near the hangar. Usually, Luke would smile, lift his hand, and use the Force to pull the snack right out. But today was strange. The Force was silent. No matter how hard he tried, nothing moved.

Luke sighed and said, “Okay… I guess I’ll do this the normal way.” He took out real credits and walked up to the vending machine.

The first vending machine was simple but friendly. Luke could see everything inside it through the glass. He could see the snacks, the little metal arms, and how they moved. A small sign said: Press a button, put in the right money, get the snack. Luke pressed the blue button, put in his credits, and BANG! His favourite snack fell down. Luke smiled. He understood this machine. He knew why it gave him that snack. This machine was interpretable. It was simple, clear, and made sense, even to a tired Jedi.

Next to it stood a new vending machine. It was shiny, quiet, and covered in blinking lights. Luke couldn’t see inside it at all. He put in the same money and pressed the same button. The machine gave strange sounds, thought for a moment, and then dropped a snack. A screen lit up and said: “I gave you this snack because you pressed this button and paid this much money.” Luke scratched his head. The snack was correct, but he didn’t really understand how the machine decided. It only explained itself after giving the snack. That was explainability.

The next day, intrigued and tired, Luke came back again. He pressed the same button and paid the same amount of money, but the machine dropped a different snack and did not match his expectation. The machine said, “I gave you this snack because of your choices, but my inside logic somehow picked something different today.” Luke felt sad and confused. “I didn’t do anything different from yesterday,” he said. And he was right. He wanted to understand how the machine worked and why it gave a different snack this time, so he could get his favourite snack next time.

Luke learned something important that day. Getting the right snack is good, but understanding the machine is even better. When machines make decisions, he must know how they work or be able to explain their choices. Because when something goes wrong, the responsibility is not with him but with those who built the machine.

To conclude, interpretability helps us see how a system works, while explainability helps us understand why it made a specific choice.
